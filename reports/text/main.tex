\begin{abstract}
This report accompanies the specification in \code{README.md} and records what
building the system established that the specification did not state, together
with what had to be corrected. Three results are structural rather than
incidental. First, the numerical noise floor sits nearly eight orders of
magnitude below the smallest non-zero score separation, so floating-point error
cannot reorder two distinctly-scored documents; every ranking instability in the
unperturbed pipeline arises from \emph{exact} ties, and therefore from the
tie-break. Second, the tolerance $\tau$ admits no single principled value, but it
does admit a derived \emph{band} across which the reported tie structure is
provably invariant. Third, a fine near-tie cannot be manufactured by editing
document text, because term-frequency normalisation makes a single-token edit a
relative perturbation of order $1/L$; the case study of \S7.4 must therefore
identify a near-tie rather than construct one. Twenty-eight addenda are recorded
in \code{docs/spec\_addenda.md}; this report summarises those that change what
may be claimed.
\end{abstract}

\section{Two implementations, one definition of correctness}

The system exists twice: a pure-Python reference and an optimised C++20 core.
The relationship between them is the central architectural commitment.

The \textbf{Python reference is normative}. It defines correctness, has no
compiled dependency, and every published number is defined by what it produces.
The \textbf{C++ core is an optimisation} required to be bit-identical, which the
test suite enforces by comparing raw bit patterns rather than tolerances. A
tolerance would silently admit the divergence under study.

Bit-identity is asserted across four reduction policies, two scoring kernels
(term-at-a-time and document-at-a-time) and three ranking operators. For a
ranking the claim is \emph{permutation identity}, which holds given four
separately-testable conditions: the sort key is injective, its inputs are
identical, the comparison relation is the same in both languages, and the build
is not fast-math.

\section{Removing floating point rather than handling it}

\subsection{The logarithm}\label{sec:log}

\code{math.log} is not correctly rounded, and \addendum{13} records the
consequence: it differs from the correctly-rounded value in $15.16\%$ of IDF
entries, and UCRT, glibc and Apple libm disagree with one another. Left alone,
Linux and Windows produce different weights, norms, scores and rankings, which
falsifies the reproducibility claim.

IDF is therefore computed once in Python via \code{decimal.Decimal.ln()} at
60~digits and rounded once to binary64. The C++ core never sees a logarithm; it
receives IDF as data. What remains is $+$, $-$, $\times$, $\div$ and $\sqrt{\ }$,
which IEEE-754 requires to be correctly rounded~\cite{ieee754-2019}, so every
conforming platform must agree.

\subsection{The tie-break}

The same move applies to ordering. Tie-break attributes are rank-encoded to
dense \code{int32} once per corpus, with direction and missing-value placement
baked into the rank rather than applied per comparison. No floating point
survives in the tie-break, so NaN cannot enter through it at all: a type-level
guarantee.

\section{The measured noise floor, and the derivation of \texorpdfstring{$\tau$}{tau}}

\S7.1 asks that $\tau$ ``exceed floating-point noise while remaining small
relative to typical score separations''. The constraint is two-sided and
qualitative: it delimits an interval and supplies no procedure for choosing a
point inside it. Deriving a single number from it would manufacture precision the
specification does not contain.

\subsection{The lower endpoint}

$\tau$ decides whether $|s_i - s_j| \le \tau$ is evidence of a real difference.
The quantity to bound is therefore the error in a \emph{margin} rather than the
error in a score. If each score carries error at most $\eta$, the margin carries
at most $2\eta$ and both signs are attainable, so $\tau_{\text{floor}} = 2\eta$.
The factor is exact, and is the same factor as in $\varepsilon_k^{\text{flip}} =
m_k/2$.

$\eta$ is measured against \code{Reduction.EXACT}, an exact-summation
expansion~\cite{shewchuk1997adaptive}, over the corpus.

The norms are recomputed under each policy rather than reused.
\code{TfidfModel.norms} is precomputed under the model's own reduction, so
varying only the scorer's policy would hold them fixed and understate $\eta$: a
dot product runs over a handful of shared terms, while a norm sums the whole
document vector, and the longer sum is where the error accumulates.

reduction-policy sweep over short queries therefore measures nothing while
appearing informative.

\subsection{The upper endpoint, and why the band is provable}

The upper endpoint is $g_{\min}$, the smallest strictly-positive adjacent score
gap observed.

\begin{proposition}[Piecewise constancy]\label{prop:piecewise}
Every $\tau$-dependent object in the implementation is piecewise constant in
$\tau$, with breakpoints among the pairwise score differences $s_a - s_b$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}[Argument]
\code{tie\_chains} cuts wherever an adjacent gap exceeds $\tau$, so it changes
only when $\tau$ crosses an adjacent gap. \code{tie\_cliques} admits an interval
whose diameter is at most $\tau$, and \code{tie\_ball}$(j,\tau)$ is delimited by
$|s_i - s_j| \le \tau$; each changes when $\tau$ crosses a difference $s_a -
s_b$. Such a difference is a sum of consecutive gaps, and a sum of gaps is not in
general a gap, so the adjacent gaps do not exhaust the breakpoints.
\end{proof}

An earlier version of this proposition put the breakpoints at observed gap values
alone. That is false for the complete-linkage objects, and measurably so: across
the swept range $\rho(\tau)$ has $129$ discontinuities, of which $18$ are at an
adjacent gap and $111$ at a clique threshold that is not one. Sweeping the gaps
alone located one in seven and interpolated the rest, so the runner now evaluates
both sets; see Figure~\ref{fig:rho}.

Consequently, if $[\tau_{\text{floor}}, g_{\min})$ contains no pairwise
difference, every $\tau$ within it yields bit-identical tie structure. It cannot
contain one: a strictly-positive difference is a sum of adjacent gaps of which at
least one is positive, so it is at least $g_{\min}$. The band is therefore empty
of breakpoints by construction and not by observation, which makes the invariance
across it weaker evidence than it first appears, as
Finding~\ref{find:plateau} records. Measured: $\eta =
5.551\times10^{-16}$, $\tau_{\text{floor}} = 1.110\times10^{-15}$, $g_{\min} =
3.709\times10^{-8}$, a band of $7.52$ decades.

This is not circular: $\tau_{\text{floor}}$ comes from arithmetic and $g_{\min}$
from the score lattice, and neither is a function of $\tau$.

\resultfigure{fig_tau_band}{0.9}{\profilecmd}{%
The admissible band for $\tau$. The lower endpoint is twice the measured noise

\begin{finding}\label{find:plateau}
Invariance across the band reflects \emph{emptiness of the score lattice}; it is
no evidence that the tie-break mechanism is robust. The plateau must always be
reported with its cause, since stating it alone invites the stronger reading,
which the data do not support.
\end{finding}

\section{A1: margins govern stability}

\S4.4 gives a sufficient condition: if every score moves by less than $m_k/2$,
the top-$k$ set is unchanged. Two facts about that bound are easy to conflate.

\paragraph{Tight in the worst case.} A witness built from dyadic rationals flips
the pair: scores $0.5$ and $0.25$, $m = 0.25$, $\varepsilon = 0.125 + 2^{-30}$,
every step exact in binary64. The bound cannot be improved.

\paragraph{Conservative on average.} Under random perturbation the adversarial
configuration is a measure-zero corner of the perturbation cube.

Reporting only the second understates the risk; reporting only the first
suggests rankings are far more fragile than they are.

The certificate is audited as a $2\times2$ table rather than an accuracy, since
accuracy would reward a certificate that always declined. Certified-and-changed
was $0$ (any other value falsifies \S4.4, and the runner exits non-zero); $51\%$
of \emph{uncertified} perturbations left the top-$k$ unchanged regardless ($296$
of $579$).
``Not certified'' must not be read as ``will change''.

\section{A2: tie-breaking causes discontinuities}

The claim that tie-break effects are independent of numerical error is earned
structurally. \code{rank\_all\_operators} shares one sorted-score object across
$\pi$, $\pi_{\text{score}}$ and $\pi_{\text{alt}}$, so no arithmetic separates
them and a disagreement admits a single cause. The runner verifies this with
an identity check before reporting anything, and aborts otherwise.
Identity rather than bit comparison: the three share one score array, so
comparing values would compare that array with itself and pass whatever
happened, including after the sharing was removed.

Against $\pi_{\text{score}}$ disagreement concentrates sharply at $k=1$:
$42.5\%$, against at most $10\%$ at every other $k$ measured. The leading
document frequently sits in an exact-tie block, so the decision-level
discontinuity lands at the rank where a recommender's output is most visible.

Against $\pi_{\text{alt}}$ it does not concentrate there at all. That pair reads
$2.5\%$ at $k=1$ and peaks at $7.5\%$ at $k=20$, which is the more ordinary
shape: the two operators differ in the priority order of the tie-break
attributes, and the deeper the cut the more tied blocks it can fall inside. The
distinction matters because it is the $k=1$ column of one pair, not a property of
tie-breaking in general, that puts the effect where users see it.

\resultfigure{fig_stratified}{0.98}{\ablationcmd}{%
The same disagreement, conditioned on the margin. A non-zero rate anywhere right

``everything tested here agreed'' are opposite claims.}{fig:stratified}

Three corrections belong here.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{The tie ball is not transitive}, so it is not a partition and
      cannot be used as one. Three objects are provided (ball, chain and
      clique), related by the chain-inflation ratio $\rho$ (\addendum{1}). The
      dyadic ladder $s_i = i\cdot 2^{-20}$ is the witness.
\item \textbf{The generalised Kendall distance $K^{(p)}$ is a near-metric rather
      than a metric}~\cite{fagin2003comparing}. An earlier claim that it
      satisfies the triangle inequality at $p = 1/2$ was wrong; violations were
      measured at every $p$ tested, and the implementation was verified against
      an independently written reference. It is used on bias grounds
      (\addendum{2}).
\item \textbf{\S4.5 does not say which reordering $\pi_{\text{alt}}$ uses.} It is
      pinned to the reversal of $\pi$'s priority. This affects published rates
      and must be stated (\addendum{15}).
\end{enumerate}

\resultfigure{fig_rho_discontinuity}{0.82}{\ablationcmd}{%
$\rho(\tau)$, the chain-inflation ratio, as the step function it is. Every riser
sits at the exact $\tau$ where $\rho$ jumps, which needs the clique thresholds as
well as the adjacent gaps (Proposition~\ref{prop:piecewise}). $\rho = 1$ says the
largest chain and the largest clique are the same size, which does not by itself
make the two groupings agree. The peak sits ten decades above the $\tau$ these
results use, where $\rho = 1$.}{fig:rho}

\section{\texorpdfstring{\S7.4}{Section 7.4}: near-ties are identified rather than constructed}

\S7.4 states that two documents are ``identified such that $|s_A - s_B| \le
\tau$''. The choice of verb is forced.

\begin{finding}\label{find:neartie}
A fine near-tie cannot be manufactured by editing document text. \S2.2 normalises
$\mathrm{tf} = \mathrm{count}/L$, so adding one token changes every term
frequency from $c/L$ to $c/(L+1)$: a \emph{relative} perturbation of $1/(L+1)$
applied to all coordinates at once. A separation of $10^{-9}$ would require a
document of roughly $10^{9}$ tokens.
\end{finding}

Adjacent gaps are instead either exactly zero ($9.5\%$ of the $3{,}570$ observed)
or above $g_{\min} = 3.7\times10^{-8}$; the interval between is empty. At fine
$\tau$ the near-tie
regime therefore \emph{is} the exact-tie regime, so \S7.3's $m_k \le \tau$
stratum measures the tie-break rather than numerical error, placing it in A2's
regime and outside A1's. Conflating the two would misattribute the cause
(\addendum{22}).

\section{The scale separation}

\begin{table}[ht]\centering
\caption{Magnitudes measured in this study.}
\label{tab:scales}
\begin{tabular}{lr}
\toprule
quantity & magnitude \\
\midrule
arithmetic noise floor $\eta$      & ${\sim}10^{-16}$ \\
smallest non-zero score gap        & ${\sim}4\times10^{-8}$ \\
typical adjacent gap               & $10^{-4}$ to $10^{-2}$ \\
corpus-edit perturbation           & $10^{-4}$ to $10^{-1}$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

Twelve orders of magnitude separate the two regimes at their nearest edges ---
the smallest corpus-edit shift measured, $1.694\times10^{-4}$, against a noise
floor of $1.665\times10^{-16}$ on that corpus --- widening to fifteen at the far
ends. Twelve is the figure the argument needs, being the closest the two regimes
ever come. Numerical error never came within seven orders of closing a real gap.
A1's bounded perturbations are therefore entirely about semantic perturbation;
floating-point error is not a threat to ranking at any scale this system operates
at. That is a positive result for the numerical
design of \S6,
and it is what leaves A1 and A2 as separable questions rather than two names for
one effect.

\section{Threats to validity}

\paragraph{Configuration dependence.} The noise floor bounds only the
configuration it was measured on. Every experiment records the model digest, and
a run against a different vocabulary or reduction is not covered by that floor.

\paragraph{Finite sampling.} $\eta$ is a maximum over a finite query grid and is
therefore a lower bound on the true worst case. It is reported as measured, with
its grid size, rather than as a proven bound.

\paragraph{Corpus dependence.} The gap distribution shares vary with corpus size,
vocabulary and query; an earlier draft quoted one configuration's table as
canonical and was corrected. The same draft claimed the interval $(0, 10^{-9})$
was invariably empty, and that does not survive either. It holds on the seeded
generator, where a test enforces it and says in its own docstring that it
generalises no further. MovieLens falsifies it: $197$ of $114{,}504$ adjacent
pairs fall below that corpus's own $\tau_{\text{floor}}$ of $4.441\times10^{-16}$
--- not the $1.110\times10^{-15}$ derived above, which belongs to the synthetic
corpus, though the count is $197$ against either threshold --- and the smallest
strictly-positive gap is $1.4\times10^{-11}$ even with the scores recomputed
exactly, so the emptiness is a property of the generator rather than of TF-IDF
(\addendum{22}).

\paragraph{Synthetic data.} The controlled results use a seeded generator.
MovieLens supplies external validity but may not be redistributed, so results on
it are marked non-reproducible-from-repository in their provenance block.

\section{Reproducing every number}

\begin{lstlisting}[language=bash]
python scripts/run_stability_profile.py   --dataset synthetic_tiny -o reports/
python scripts/run_tie_break_ablations.py --dataset synthetic_tiny \
       --tau 4.8e-13 -o reports/
python scripts/make_figures.py --reports reports/
python scripts/snapshot.py    # the cross-platform reproducibility digest
\end{lstlisting}

Each runner writes a result envelope whose digest is taken over the payload and
the parameters block together, with volatile fields stripped, so a rerun on the
same data yields the same string and
a published number can be checked by comparing one hexadecimal value. Continuous
integration computes \code{snapshot.py} on Linux, macOS and Windows, at three
optimisation levels and under both backends, and requires every digest to be
identical.
